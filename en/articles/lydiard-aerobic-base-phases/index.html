The Lydiard method: aerobic base, hills and sharpening over 28 weeks

In brief

  • Arthur Lydiard organises preparation in phases: aerobic base, hills and speed development, sharpening, taper. The source describes an ideal 28-week plan.
  • The aerobic base is the most important phase: three long runs a week, at a steady pace and not “flat out”.
  • The source is a 2000 magazine article about Lydiard's lectures, not his books. The source consulted is not a controlled comparison of the method.

The four phases

PhaseLengthContent
1. Aerobic base12 weeksThree long runs a week at a steady pace; easy running on the other days; one 5–10 km tempo run below threshold
2. Hills and speed8 weeksFirst 4: hill circuit three times a week and leg speed. Next 4: track sessions three times a week, about 3 miles of fast running
3. Sharpening6 weeksOne relaxed long run, one low-volume anaerobic session, a weekly time trial
4. Taper10 daysRun every day, with little fast volume and light long runs

Lengths and contents are those of the article.1

The aerobic base

For Lydiard a runner's anaerobic limit is largely set by their aerobic potential: that is why the base decides the rest of the program. The long runs go at more than recovery pace, but without lactate build-up. To find the pace: on a flat course run out strongly for 15 minutes, then run back. If the return takes the same time or slightly less, the pace is right. If it takes longer, you started too fast. The goal is to be “pleasantly tired”.1

Illustrative example

DaySession in the base phase (from the article)
Three daysone 2-hour run and two 1.5-hour runs
One dayrun with some light changes of pace
One day5–10 km tempo below lactate threshold
Other daysshort easy runs

If you lack the volume base to start like this, the source says to reduce times and distances and build gradually.1

Hills and speed

The hill circuit includes bounding uphill, fast downhill running and sprints. Ideally on a hill with a flat 200–400 m section for sprints, a 200–300 m rise for bounding and a recovery section. Total about an hour, plus warm-up and cool-down, three days a week. On the remaining days you work on leg speed: 10 repetitions of 120–150 m.1

Sharpening

Three fixed sessions: the relaxed long run; an anaerobic session (five laps of a 400 m track alternating 50 m of sprint and 50 m strong but easy); and a weekly time trial at or below the race distance, with splits recorded to find weak points.1

Limits

  • The source is a 2000 Runner's World article that summarises Lydiard's lectures. His books were not read.
  • The phases are for a goal with a final race and a 28-week plan: the article does not explain how to adapt them for people with less time.
  • The base volume is high. The source consulted is not a controlled comparison of the method. The statements about aerobic potential and the anaerobic limit are Lydiard's.
  • The method is described for running. It does not apply to cycling.
