Electrical stimulation, EMS and TENS: strength, recovery and pain, what the studies say

In brief

  • "Electrical stimulator" covers devices with different goals. The studies treat muscle stimulation (NMES or EMS, which makes the muscle contract), TENS (which acts on pain with a tingling sensation) and whole-body stimulation separately.
  • For strength, at matched training volume, training with stimulation gives similar gains to voluntary training. It does not show it does better.
  • For recovery the evidence is thin and not convincing for later performance. For the endurance of a healthy athlete the data are scarce.
  • For pain, TENS has moderate-certainty evidence in one large meta-analysis, but very low quality in the Cochrane reviews on chronic pain. It is not about training soreness.
  • Precautions depend on the type of use: for whole-body stimulation the sources describe severe cases after the first sessions; with a pacemaker or defibrillator, ask a doctor.

Three goals, three questions

When asking "does it work?" you have to say for what: training a muscle, recovering after effort, or reducing pain. The reviews below answer different questions and cannot be added together. No device, brand or model was assessed, and we give no intensities, frequencies or durations: study protocols vary too much and are not instructions. The rest of Recovery is in the second guide and the first.

What they are

  • NMES / EMS: current applied through the skin to a muscle group to cause contractions, even strong ones (usually between 10 and 60% of maximal voluntary force). Used to train strength in healthy people and athletes and in rehabilitation.1
  • TENS: mild currents through electrodes on the skin to relieve pain. In acute pain trials it produces a strong but non-painful tingling in the area.11
  • Whole-body stimulation (WB-EMS): stimulates the main muscle groups at the same time, sometimes at very high intensity for each area.6

Strength and training

  • What it shows: A 2023 review of 10 studies (174 healthy people) reports strength gains in every study, but no improvement in strength-related functional measures. The authors could not establish the best duration, intensity or frequency because protocols differed.2 A 2022 meta-analysis of 19 studies compares stimulation with voluntary training at matched volume: practically equal strength gains (difference 0.02, interval -0.20 to 0.25).3
  • Athletes: A network meta-analysis (36 studies, 1092 trained participants) finds that strength, jump and sprint can increase with adequate programmes, with effects that differ widely by method. The largest effects appear with stimulation superimposed on voluntary exercise. For aerobic capacity the interval includes no effect.4
  • Limits: Small studies and mixed protocols. The results hold for the protocols studied, not for any device.

Endurance

  • What it shows: A 2016 narrative review reports that high-frequency stimulation raises strength but not muscle endurance and has little influence on functional endurance (for example maximal oxygen uptake). At low frequency and with very long sessions, 3 studies show improvements in functional endurance, but data are scarce. Effects are larger in inactive patients than in healthy, active people.1
  • Limits: It is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis. It does not point to a useful use for people who already run or ride.

Recovery

  • What it shows: A 2014 systematic review of 13 studies (healthy people) finds few favourable results: 3 studies on perceived pain, 3 on lactate, 1 on creatine kinase, 1 on a performance measure. The authors conclude there may be subjective benefits, but the evidence is not convincing for improving later performance.5
  • Limits: A 2014 review with a search to 2012: newer studies were not read.

Pain: TENS

  • What it shows: A 2022 meta-analysis of 381 studies (24,532 participants) finds lower pain during or right after TENS than placebo, with moderate certainty. Adverse events were poorly reported, generally mild and not different from controls.9 A 2019 Cochrane overview on chronic pain instead rates the quality of the evidence very low and concludes neither for nor against TENS.10 For acute pain a 2015 Cochrane review (19 trials, 1346 people) finds TENS may reduce pain intensity in some patients, with weak evidence; a small number had itching or redness under the pads.11 The sources do not agree on certainty, and we say so.
  • Limits: The studies measure pain during or right after use. The acute studies are about medical procedures, not training soreness. Pain that persists or has an unclear cause needs assessment.

Safety and precautions

  • Whole body: A 2023 position statement recalls that cases of severe rhabdomyolysis were reported mostly after the first application, and recommends supervision, qualified trainers, medical history and contraindication checks before the session, preparation of beginners and recovery periods between sessions.6 A 2024 German consensus revises the contraindications for non-medical commercial use: arterial disease and neurological disease with epilepsy remain absolute; diabetes and tumours were moved to relative contraindications. The 2016 list also included pacemakers, electric implants, arrhythmia, pregnancy, untreated hypertension, and abdominal and inguinal hernia.7 These are lists for a specific use, not for any device.
  • Pacemakers and defibrillators: A systematic review (search to August 2016) concludes that stimulation of the thigh muscles is probably safe, but with small studies (the largest in 27 patients), and reports two cases of inappropriate shocks after abdominal stimulation. The authors recommend caution and checking the device at first use.8 With an implanted device, ask a doctor before any use.

How to read an offer

Check which outcome is claimed (strength, recovery, pain) and whether the cited study covers that outcome in people like you: many studies are on patients or untrained people. "Electrical stimulator" does not say whether the device causes contractions or acts on pain. The strength reviews cover specific protocols, not off-the-shelf devices. Here we do not compare devices and do not recommend any.

What is not covered

Specific devices and models, use in medical rehabilitation, other uses of current (for example for circulation) and studies newer than the reviews cited.

How we reviewed it

Document review of sources on muscle stimulation, TENS and whole-body stimulation. Abstracts and main sections read; not every full text or primary study. No device tested or compared. No WOLF LAB tests.

Limits. Different goals, reviews cannot be added together. Mixed protocols, no settings given. The reviews on recovery (2014) and pacemakers (search to 2016) are dated; newer studies not read. The TENS sources disagree on certainty. The contraindication lists cover commercial whole-body use. No device assessed. Not medical advice.
