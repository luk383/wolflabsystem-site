USRPT in swimming: short race-pace repetitions and what the sources say

In brief

  • USRPT (ultra-short race-pace training) is a method proposed by Brent Rushall in 2011: many short repetitions at the swimmer’s own race speed, with 15–25 second rests.
  • The systematic review by Nugent and colleagues (2019) found no peer-reviewed published USRPT interventions. That may have changed since: the review dates from 2019.
  • The recommendations to exclude weights, cross-training, paces below race pace and technique drills are called controversial and unsupported by the review authors.

What it is

According to the review, a USRPT session usually has 20 to 50 repetitions over 15–100 m (5–70 s) at the speed of the swimmer’s best race performance, with 15–25 s rests.1

Illustrative example from the source

A typical example reported by the review, attributed to Rushall: 20 × 50 m at 200 m race speed with 20 s rest. Work volume is 1000 m. It is not a prescription: in the method, if the swimmer cannot hold the set speed (a “failure”) the next repetition is skipped; three failures, or two in a row, end the set.1

The stated principles

PrincipleHow Rushall describes it (as reported by the review)
SpecificityRace-specific technique, psychology and conditioning. Technique at race speed is the most important part.
OverloadThe number of repetitions is raised first, then the speed.
RecoveryThe session adapts to the individual: a failure gives more recovery by skipping a repetition.
IndividualityThe number of repetitions depends on the swimmer.

These are the statements of the method’s author.1

What the review says

  • Of 1347 studies found, 15 were read in full and all were excluded because the intervention was not USRPT.
  • Rushall’s literature on his own site was not peer reviewed and, for the authors, largely anecdotal.
  • For the authors USRPT is a derivative of high-intensity training (HIT), for which studies exist: an earlier review found 7 studies, 6 with physiological improvements and 4 with performance improvements, but mostly short (4–6.5 weeks).
  • National and international level swimmers do about 6–16% of training as HIT; the rest is low and moderate intensity, strength and other work.

The authors suggest using USRPT as one method inside a periodized programme, not as a replacement for everything.1

More recent studies

Later work measures acute responses to a single session: for example 20 × 25 m at 100 m pace raised lactate to 13.6 mmol/L and heart rate to 188 bpm, and a USRPT-like session gave lower lactate than 10 × 100 m. These are responses to one session, not proof that the method works better over time.2

Limits and safety

  • For young swimmers, the volumes the author gives (3–7 sessions a week for ages 8–12, 8–9 for 14 and over) are judged by the review as potentially harmful: repeated high-intensity fatigue raises the risk of dropout and overtraining.
  • Events of 200 m and longer depend heavily on the aerobic system; only the 50 m and 100 m are mainly anaerobic.
  • There are two sources: a 2019 review and a later study on acute responses (18 swimmers around 13 years old). No search was made for newer studies on long-term performance.
