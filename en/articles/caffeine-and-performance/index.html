Caffeine and performance: studied doses, safety and sleep

In brief

  • Caffeine can improve some performance outcomes, but responses and adverse effects vary. It is not necessary to train or race.
  • 3–6 mg/kg is a common research range, not an automatic dose. ISSN notes effective doses may be as low as about 2 mg/kg.
  • Performance-study quantities are not the same as EFSA safety references. All sources and sleep matter.

What ISSN says

The 2021 ISSN position reports acute benefits in many, not all, studies. Aerobic endurance has some of the most consistent findings, but magnitude varies between people. A frequently studied range is 3–6 mg/kg; the minimum effective dose remains uncertain and may be around 2 mg/kg. Very high doses, such as 9 mg/kg, increase adverse effects without being required for benefit.1

The most common timing in studies is about 60 minutes before exercise, but form matters: caffeinated gum does not necessarily have the same timing as a capsule. This is not a reason to take more products if an effect does not feel immediate.

Performance and safety are different questions

Source and contextReported quantityHow to read it
ISSN, performance studiesOften 3–6 mg/kgResearch range, not an individual safety guarantee
EFSA, healthy adultsUp to 200 mg as a single dose from all sourcesNo safety concerns in assessed conditions, also before intense exercise in normal environmental conditions
EFSA, healthy non-pregnant adultsUp to 400 mg spread throughout the dayNot a target or guarantee for every person
EFSA, sleepEven 100 mg near bedtimeMay affect sleep duration and patterns in some adults

EFSA’s assessment does not cover diseases or medicine interactions. Healthy-adult references should not automatically be extended to minors, pregnancy or breastfeeding, or people with cardiac conditions, anxiety or other conditions. These require discussion with a healthcare professional, not a table calculation.2

Calculation examples, not recommended doses

Hypothetical weight2 mg/kg3 mg/kg6 mg/kg
60 kg120 mg180 mg360 mg
70 kg140 mg210 mg420 mg
80 kg160 mg240 mg480 mg

At 70 kg, 3 mg/kg is 210 mg, already above EFSA’s 200-mg single-dose reference. Six mg/kg is 420 mg, above its 400-mg daily reference before counting coffee or tea. This does not make 201 mg a toxicity threshold: it means performance ranges do not automatically authorise any intake.12

A hypothetical label example: an 80-mg serving, a 100-mg serving and a 50-mg gel total 230 mg. Count all sources rather than assuming every espresso has a fixed dose. EFSA notes variation by preparation, beans and serving size; a coffee count is not a precise unit.2

Sleep, stomach and individual response

Anxiety, palpitations, gastrointestinal problems and disturbed sleep can offset the intended benefit. ISSN describes variability involving metabolism and individual response. There is no single cutoff time for everyone. For evening races, consider sleep costs alongside potential benefit, not afterwards.12

Caffeine does not replace recovery, carbohydrate or hydration. ISSN considers effects under sleep deprivation, but this does not establish that caffeine removes the need to sleep.

Read a product without stacking blindly

  • Check milligrams per serving and servings per packet. One gel is not a standard dose.
  • Add coffee, tea, drinks and other supplements already consumed.
  • Do not automatically add a mid-race caffeinated gel after a pre-start dose: the total is part of the decision.
  • Do not try a new product on race day. Past tolerance does not guarantee no adverse effects.
  • If symptoms, medical conditions or medicines are involved, do not use the table to decide alone.
