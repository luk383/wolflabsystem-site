Cycling to train for a marathon: what transfers and what doesn't

In short

  • Cycling trains the central engine (heart, blood volume, aerobic capacity) but only partly transfers to running.
  • It doesn't train tendons, bones or running economy: long runs and marathon-pace work have to be run.
  • Use it to add easy volume and to recover, especially in the base phase. In the last 8–10 weeks, running comes first.

What carries over from bike to run

Running and cycling share most of the aerobic engine: a heart that pumps more with each beat, more blood, more capillaries. That is why people who ride a lot come to running with a good base. The transfer is partial, though: a 1994 review of VO₂max concludes that some transfer exists, but cross-training effects never exceed those of sport-specific training, and specificity matters more in highly trained athletes. It is an old review and covers VO₂max only, not marathon performance.1

The reason is in the muscles. On the bike the work is concentrated in the quads, with almost no eccentric phase and no impact. In running every stride absorbs several times your body weight, uses calves and glutes far more and needs tendon elasticity that cycling doesn't train.2

What the bike can't give you

  • Structural resilience: Achilles tendon, bones and plantar fascia only adapt to running load.
  • Running economy: how much energy you spend at a given pace depends on the movement, and the movement is trained by running.
  • Race specificity: holding marathon pace for 30 km and beyond is an adaptation only long runs provide.

The risk of overdoing it

A ride isn't free. If it is long or hard it takes energy and recovery away from the next day's run. John Davis, a running researcher and coach, warns about exactly this: a lot of aerobic work on the bike can leave you tired for the sessions that matter. There is no reliable conversion factor between hours on the bike and running kilometres: think in time and perceived effort, and count everything in the week's load.

How to use it in each phase

PhaseRole of the bike
Base (winter and spring)Easy aerobic volume in place of some easy runs. Useful to grow without adding too much impact.
BuildEasy rides on the days after hard sessions or long runs, to recover while moving.
Specific (last 8–10 weeks)Only short, light active recovery. Running kilometres come first.
Injury or load-related painMaintains aerobic fitness while running is reduced.

Keep most of the work at low intensity, in both sports. It is the model the best endurance athletes have followed for years: lots of easy volume and few truly hard sessions.3

What we know and what we don't

That the transfer is partial comes from the cited reviews, though the main one dates from 1994. What is missing are solid studies on how much cycling is optimal for an amateur marathoner: the practical advice above comes from physiology and coaching experience, not from direct trials.
