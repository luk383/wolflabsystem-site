Carbohydrate and fluid on the bike: two separate calculations

Cycling nutrition3 min readPublished October 29, 2026

Start with the session

A short, easy ride does not automatically require a sports drink. Duration, intensity, previous meals and opportunities to stop change the question. AIS separates carbohydrate intake from fluid needs: a bottle can contribute to both, but the two goals do not always match. [1]

AIS reference ranges, not a personal dose

ActivityAIS practical reference
Brief, under 45 minutesCarbohydrate during exercise generally unnecessary
Sustained high intensity, 45–75 minutesSmall amounts, including oral exposure
Endurance, 1–2.5 hours30–60 g carbohydrate per hour
Very long, over 2.5–3 hoursUp to 90 g/hour; multiple transportable carbohydrates

These are practical guidance ranges, not an instruction to reach the maximum. The source’s duration bands are not a continuous formula: they do not automatically produce an exact dose at 50 minutes or 2 hours 45. Higher intakes need tolerance and practice, not a first trial on event day. [1]

Worked example: a 6% bottle

A drink containing 6 g per 100 mL provides 30 g in 500 mL and 45 g in 750 mL. Consuming 500 mL plus a gel labelled as 25 g in one hour gives 55 g carbohydrate. These are illustrative sums, not instructions to drink 500 mL/hour or use that gel. Check the actual serving on the label, not just the scoop count.

AIS describes 4–8 g/100 mL as the range for many sports drinks, not every product. Mixing a powder with less water changes concentration. Adding gels changes total intake even if the bottle is unchanged. [1]

Do not drink more just to reach the carbohydrate target

If carbohydrate needs are high but fluid needs lower, the sources can be separated rather than forcing extra drink. Conversely, a carbohydrate-free drink does not meet a fuel target. The hyponatraemia consensus identifies excess fluid intake as the main preventable mechanism: added sodium does not make drinking beyond losses safe. Thirst is a reference for preventing excess, not a reason to ignore heat, symptoms or water access. [1][2]

Test the plan and record the outcome

  • Count all carbohydrate from bottles, gels and foods without counting servings twice.
  • Practise the products, concentration and intake method intended for the event.
  • Record duration, conditions, actual intake and tolerance, not only the original plan.

Nausea or discomfort does not automatically mean more salt or a different powder is needed. AIS discusses variables including concentration, amount and individual tolerance. A useful plan is repeatable and manageable, not simply the one with the largest number. [1]

Sources and limits

  1. Australian Institute of Sport, Sports drinks. Practical guidance: activity ranges, composition and tolerance.
  2. Third International Exercise-Associated Hyponatraemia Consensus, 2015. Consensus on preventing fluid excess, not a personal recipe.
