How many carbs per hour in a marathon

In short

  • For races of 1 to 2.5 hours, aim for 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour. Beyond 2.5 hours you can go up to 90 g per hour, but only with glucose and fructose mixes.
  • In the 36–48 hours before the race, carb loading means 10–12 g per kg of body weight per day.
  • Your gut can be trained: rehearse amounts and products in long runs, never for the first time on race day.

Why carbohydrate matters so much

At marathon pace your body burns mostly carbohydrate. Glycogen stores in muscle and liver are limited, and for many amateurs they run low in the last 10–12 km. That is when pace collapses even though the legs still feel fit.

Taking carbohydrate while running does not refill the stores, but it slows how fast they empty and keeps blood glucose stable. That is why the main sports nutrition guidelines recommend it for any endurance event over an hour.1

How much, by race duration

Race durationCarbs per hourType
Under 1 hourNot neededA mouth rinse at most
1–2.5 hours30–60 gGlucose or maltodextrin is enough
Over 2.5 hoursUp to 90 gGlucose and fructose mixes

The 60 g per hour ceiling with glucose alone comes from the gut: the transporter that absorbs it saturates. Fructose uses a different route, so a mix of the two lets you absorb more with fewer stomach problems.2

Some professionals now go beyond 90 g per hour. A 2026 review argues the ceiling could rise to 120 g per hour, at least in trained athletes, while the 120–200 g/h seen in the field is not yet backed by studies.5 That is data on athletes who have trained for it over time, not on amateurs: for amateurs it is not a good starting point.

Before the race

Loading. In the 36–48 hours before the marathon, guidelines suggest 10–12 g of carbohydrate per kg per day.1 For a 70 kg runner that is 700–840 g a day, more than it sounds. Cutting back on fibre and fat makes it easier to manage without feeling heavy.

Breakfast. 1–4 g per kg, 1 to 4 hours before the start, with smaller amounts the closer you get to the gun.1

In practice: a 3:30 marathon

With a 3 hour 30 minute goal and 60 g per hour, leaving out the first 20 minutes, you need about 190 g of carbohydrate during the race. With 25 g gels that is 8 gels, one roughly every 24 minutes. If you take sports drink at aid stations, every cup counts: you can drop to 5–6 gels.

Moving towards 75–90 g per hour can help, but only if you have rehearsed it several times in long runs at race pace.

Training the gut

The stomach adapts: taking carbohydrate regularly in training increases how much you can absorb and reduces discomfort.3 Use the long runs of the last 8–10 weeks to test the hourly amount, the gel brand and the timing, ideally with the same products you will find at the aid stations.
