In brief

  • Daniels describes five kinds of running: easy and long (E/L), marathon pace (MP), threshold (T), intervals (I) and repetitions (R). Each has a different purpose.
  • Paces come from VDOT, a fitness value taken from a recent race. The source advises not to run faster than the assigned pace.
  • These are the author's ideas, not the result of a controlled study. The paces for each VDOT are in the author's tables, not reproduced here.

The five types of training

Daniels starts from goals: move more blood and oxygen to the muscles, use it better, raise VO₂max, shift lactate threshold to a faster speed, improve speed and lower the energy cost of running.1

TypeIntensity describedWhat it is for (per the author)
E / L: easy and longabout 70% of VO₂max (75% of HRmax)Recovery, cell and heart adaptations. Time matters more than pace.
MP: marathon paceMarathon race pace, about 20–30 s/mile slower than thresholdOnly for people preparing for a marathon.
T: thresholdabout 88% of VO₂max, “comfortably hard”Shift lactate threshold. Neither slower nor faster works as well.
I: intervalsAt the velocity of VO₂max (98–100% of HRmax); each rep up to 5 minutesStimulate VO₂max without running all out.
R: repetitionsFaster than I, chosen from the race you are training forEconomy and speed, not VO₂max or threshold.

Illustrative example from the author

Daniels suggests aiming at 98–100% of HRmax, not always 100%. If a runner reaches HRmax at 6:00 per mile (about 3:44 per km), then 5:50 or faster will also give it. But that is too fast for the purpose: no better results and more fatigue. Heart rate does not tell these paces apart: what matters is speed at VO₂max.1 Two runners with the same velocity at VO₂max have the same T and I paces, but different R if they train for races of different length.

VDOT

VDOT is an “adjusted” VO₂max, taken from a performance over one distance. With that value you read the paces for E/L, MP, T, I and R. The races used must have been run in similar conditions (terrain, wind, temperature, altitude). To predict the marathon, a 15 to 25 km race is better than a mile or a 5 km. Daniels writes not to try to beat the assigned paces: to train harder you must first earn a better race.2

A 24-week season

PhaseWeeksContent
FI: foundation and injury prevention1–6Easy running
IQ: initial quality7–12Repetitions (R): mechanics, economy, some speed
TQ: transition quality13–18The hardest phase: mostly long intervals
FQ: final quality19–24A fair amount of threshold, few or no R or I

Four phases of six weeks, as the author writes. With less time, Daniels offers a priority system: with 6 weeks, 3 of FI and 3 of FQ; with 10 weeks, 3 of FI, 1 of IQ, 3 of TQ and 3 of FQ.3

“No man’s land”

For the author, the intensities between T and E (except MP), between I and T and between R and I are too easy or too hard for the goal: he calls them “quality-junk”. Every session should have a precise purpose.1

Limits

  • These are 2000 articles written by the author, published on a coaches' site. The book Daniels' Running Formula is not among the sources of this sheet.
  • This sheet does not reproduce the VDOT pace tables or give calculated paces: for those, use the original source.
  • The source consulted describes the system without evaluating it as a whole in a controlled study. The VO₂max and HRmax percentages are the author's.
  • It is a running method. It should not be applied to cycling, where the source says nothing: for the bike see the cycling sheets.
