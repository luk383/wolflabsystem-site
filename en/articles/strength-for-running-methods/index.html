Strength for running: studied methods and protocols

Strength and recovery8 min readPublished October 28, 2026
On this page
  1. In short
  2. What was measured
  3. The method families, kept separate
  4. Limits
  5. Sources

In short

  • In middle- and long-distance runners, strength work tends to improve running economy. VO₂max and lactate threshold usually do not change.
  • Methods are not equivalent: heavy loads, plyometrics and combined methods showed small or moderate effects; submaximal loads and isometrics did not, in one meta-analysis.
  • The protocols below are the ones used in studies, with wide ranges. They are not a personal plan, and study quality is moderate.

What was measured

The systematic review by Blagrove and colleagues included 24 studies of runners with at least 6 months of experience, with interventions of at least 4 weeks and a control group that only ran. Running economy was measured in 20 studies and generally improved by 2-8% compared with controls, though not always. Time trials between 1.5 and 10 km and speed qualities also tended to improve. VO₂max, velocity at VO₂max, lactate and body composition generally did not change. Methodological quality was moderate (PEDro 4-6 out of 10).1

An umbrella review (17 reviews) confirms the effect on running economy and no significant change in VO₂max, but notes that many of the reviews had low or critically low confidence.3

The method families, kept separate

Llanos-Lagos and colleagues (studies up to November 2022) compared methods on running economy. Programmes lasted from 6 to 24 weeks, with one to four sessions per week.2

MethodDefinition usedEffect on running economy
High loadsat least 80% of 1RMSmall improvement. Larger at higher speeds and in those with higher VO₂max.
Plyometricsjumps and boundsSmall improvement, seen at speeds up to 12 km/h.
Combined methodstwo or more methodsModerate improvement. Low certainty of evidence.
Submaximal loads40-79% of 1RMNo improvement versus controls.
Isometricsstatic contractionsNo improvement versus controls.

Certainty of evidence was moderate for high loads, submaximal loads, plyometrics and isometrics. This does not show that methods without an effect are useless for other goals: it only measures running economy. An earlier review by Rønnestad and Mujika says running economy improves with both heavy and explosive strength work in trained runners.4

How the protocols were built

Ranges taken from the studies in the Blagrove review. They are tested protocols, not recommendations.1

TypePrescription in the studies
Heavy strength2-6 sets of 3-10 repetitions per exercise, at loads above 70% of 1RM or to failure.
Plyometrics1-6 exercises, 1-6 sets of 4-10 repetitions, 30 to 228 ground contacts per session.
Sprintsshort distances (20-150 m), 4-12 sets at maximal intensity.
Frequencythe review's conclusions say two or three sessions per week with a variety of modalities are likely to help.

Most studies applied progressive overload and some used periodised models. These are pooled ranges: they are not a typical week and do not say which combination is best.

Limits

  • Studies vary a lot in level, duration and method. The same review warns that general recommendations should be applied with caution.1
  • The outcomes are running economy and time trials up to 10 km. An effect on the marathon is not shown.
  • None of these sources says when to stop near a race, or how to place strength next to a threshold week. For interference with aerobic work see Strength for runners and cyclists.
  • Technique and progression need to be learned with competent help. The Wolf Lab exercise catalogue is a list, not an execution guide.

Sources

  3. Ramos-Campo et al. The effect of strength training on endurance performance determinants in middle- and long-distance endurance athletes: an umbrella review. Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2025. PubMed. Abstract read.
