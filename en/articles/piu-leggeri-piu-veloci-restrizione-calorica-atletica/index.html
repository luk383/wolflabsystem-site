Does lighter mean faster? A new systematic review on dietary restriction in athletics

Nutrition5 min readPublished November 27, 2026
On this page
  1. Why it matters
  2. What was searched
  3. What emerged
  4. How to read it
  5. Limits
  6. Sources

At a glance

  • The systematic review by Bedrač and Iskra (Nutrients 2026, 18(23), 3904) included 20 reports from 16 studies and about 234 athletes, only 14.5% women, covering prescribed energy restriction, low energy availability, time-restricted eating and Ramadan fasting during continued training.1
  • No exposure category showed a between-condition performance benefit. Body mass and fat mass often fell; bone-formation markers dropped within days of restriction. Certainty of evidence was rated low to very low.1
  • The authors conclude that the available evidence does not show that dietary restriction improves performance, and that lower body mass is not in itself evidence of benefit.1

Why it matters

In distance running and race walking, the energetic cost of carrying body mass feeds the idea that lighter means faster. The authors note that classic experimental work on added load supports it on energetic grounds, but that there is little prospective evidence that losing weight improves performance in highly trained athletes, and that neither an optimal rate of loss nor a protein intake that protects performance and physiological function has been established for athletics.1

What was searched

The authors searched PubMed, Scopus, SPORTDiscus and Web of Science up to 30 September 2026, following PRISMA 2020. They included studies of trained adults in weight-sensitive events, with a comparator condition or baseline, where restriction or fasting took place during continued training. Quality was appraised with CASP (one study high, eleven moderate, four low), risk of bias in randomised studies with RoB 2, and certainty with GRADE.1

What emerged

On performance, elite race walkers improved 10,000 m performance after energy restriction followed by refuelling, but not more than under higher energy availability; both groups were refuelled for about 24 hours before racing, so performance was measured after restriction, not during it.1 In the only controlled Ramadan comparison with race-distance testing, 5000 m performance deteriorated in the fasting group while the control group stayed stable, with no statistically significant change in body mass or reported energy intake.1

On body composition and markers: body mass and fat mass commonly decreased; significant fat-free-mass reductions appear only in bioimpedance-based studies. Bone-formation markers fell within days of restriction, while bone resorption, iron regulation, endocrine and recovery responses varied with protocol and timing.1

How to read it

The authors separate what is measured during restriction from what is measured after refuelling, and warn that a non-significant performance change is not evidence of physiological tolerance. Lower body mass alone does not demonstrate benefit.1 The bone markers cited are short-term indicators, not direct measures of skeletal strength or injury risk.1

Limits

We read the review's abstract, introduction and main discussion sections, not the individual studies it includes; figures are as reported by the authors. It is a narrative synthesis of a few small, very different studies; certainty is low or very low and women are a minority (14.5%). The findings do not cover prolonged or repeated restriction across a season, or weight-category sports. This is not medical or dietary advice: for decisions on weight and eating during training, see a qualified professional.

Sources

  1. Bedrač L, Iskra S, Does Lighter Mean Faster? Dietary Restriction and Fasting in Athletics: A Systematic Review, Nutrients 2026, 18(23), 3904 (open access), abstract, introduction and discussion consulted on 27/11/2026.
