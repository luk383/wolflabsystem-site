Strength for runners and cyclists: interference or advantage?

Strength and recoveryStrong evidence6 min readPublished October 26, 2026

In short

  • In already trained endurance athletes, adding strength work doesn't reduce gains in aerobic capacity.
  • Heavy loads and plyometrics improve running and cycling economy and performance.
  • Two sessions a week in the base phase, one to maintain in the specific phase, away from key workouts.

The fear of interference

For years people worried that strength work would "steal" endurance adaptations. A 2024 meta-analysis brought some order: only in untrained people did combined training slightly reduce VO₂max gains. In trained or highly trained endurance athletes the effect didn't appear. There was a small interference on lower-body strength, in men but not in women.1

For an amateur marathoner or cyclist who trains consistently, the risk of losing aerobic engine is minimal.

What you gain

A systematic review of middle and long-distance runners found that strength training improves running economy and time-trial performance while helping to maintain aerobic capacity.2 Another review reaches similar conclusions for cycling and running, with a practical detail: heavy loads with few reps work better than many light reps.3

On top of that, stronger muscles and tendons cope better with rising mileage, although direct evidence on this point is weaker.

  • How often: two sessions a week in the base phase, one in the final weeks to maintain.
  • When: away from key workouts. Leaving at least 6 hours between an endurance and a strength session helps; avoid heavy legs in the 48 hours before a long run or race-pace session.
  • Getting started: two or three weeks with moderate loads to learn the movements, then build up.
  • Taper: stop heavy lifting in the 10 days before the marathon.
