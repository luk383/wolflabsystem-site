Sleep and recovery: extension, naps and evidence limits

In brief

  • The AASM/SRS seven-hour recommendation is an adult-health reference, not the ideal dose for every athlete.
  • Sleep extension and naps show promise, but small, varied studies cannot guarantee faster races.
  • Time in bed is not time asleep. Persistent problems require understanding the cause, not chasing a device score.

How much: a reference and individual need

The 2015 AASM/SRS consensus recommends adults aged 18–60 sleep at least seven hours regularly for health. It does not establish that seven hours suffice for every athlete or that everyone needs ten. Duration, quality, timing, regularity and absence of sleep disorders all matter.1

The Walsh 2021 athlete consensus recommends an individual approach: load, early training, evening competition, travel and non-sport commitments can reduce sleep opportunity. A 7–9-hour window is not one solution for everyone.2

The basketball study and its limits

ItemMah 2011
Participants11 male college basketball players; mean age 19.4 years
Comparison2–4 habitual weeks followed by 5–7 extension weeks; no traditional control group
TargetAt least 10 hours in bed; naps encouraged when schedules limited night-time sleep
Measured sleepMean objective increase 110.9 minutes per night
OutcomesImproved sprint, shooting and vigilance measures; not marathon performance

The study used actigraphy and diaries, with repeated sport measures in training. A small sample without a parallel control group cannot separate all changes due to practice, season or other factors. Ten hours in bed was a protocol target, not a requirement for every reader to sleep ten hours.3

What the 2023 review adds

Cunha and colleagues reviewed 25 intervention studies from 2011–2021, searched through May 2022. Night-time extension and naps were the most promising strategies for physical or cognitive performance, but high-quality evidence was limited. Extension studies reported 46–113 additional minutes in athletes habitually sleeping about seven hours. This is not a dose to add automatically every night.4

The same review found no clear performance/recovery effects for some sleep-hygiene strategies, device removal and cold-water immersion, based on few studies. A habit can be reasonable without being a proven performance booster. No fixed number of seconds per race is promised for each extra sleep hour.

Naps: opportunity, duration and waking

The 2021 consensus considers short naps under 30 minutes when time is limited, timed in the early afternoon. The 2023 review also includes 20–90-minute naps in different settings: these findings are not equivalent or proof that longer is always better. Sleep inertia, temporary grogginess, may follow waking.24

The consensus advises avoiding naps if they interfere with falling asleep at night. Adding caffeine to a nap is not necessary; sleep responses vary. See caffeine and performance for quantities and limits.

Example: opportunity is not actual sleep

23:00 to 07:00 gives eight hours in bed. If falling asleep takes 30 minutes and another 20 are spent awake overnight, estimated sleep is 7 hours 10 minutes. A 20-minute nap raises the estimated 24-hour total to 7 hours 30 minutes. This is illustrative arithmetic, not clinical measurement or everyone’s ideal sleep amount.

A diary for at least one week can record timing, waking, naps, caffeine and daytime sleepiness. The 2021 consensus presents diaries as a simple observation tool with self-report limits. A wearable may reveal trends but cannot diagnose a disorder or measure sleep stages with certainty.2

When timing is not the only problem

Persistent insomnia, marked sleepiness, waking or recovery difficulties warrant appropriate assessment. The athlete consensus distinguishes screening from diagnosis and recommends identifying underlying disorders before generic advice: forcing more time in bed is not automatically helpful with insomnia.12

One difficult night need not become a competition to sleep. Consider context and the wider period without diagnosing or deciding training load from one score.
