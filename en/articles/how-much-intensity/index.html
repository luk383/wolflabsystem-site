Polarised or pyramidal: how much intensity you really need

In short

  • Around 80% of sessions, or more, should be at low intensity: you can talk without effort.
  • The rest is split between threshold work and hard work. Both polarised and pyramidal models work.
  • The most common amateur mistake is running or riding at moderate intensity too often.

Three zones, not seven

To think about distribution, three zones are enough. Below the first threshold (zone 1) you can talk easily. Between the first and second threshold (zone 2) you speak in short phrases: that's marathon pace and steady running. Above the second threshold (zone 3) you can only manage a few words: that's fast intervals.

What the best athletes do

Studying high-level rowers, cross-country skiers, runners and cyclists, Stephen Seiler found a consistent pattern: about 80% of sessions at low intensity and 20% at high intensity.1 Hence the name "polarised": little work in the middle zone.

A systematic review of elite distance runners found that many actually follow a pyramidal model: lots of zone 1, a smaller share of zone 2, even less zone 3.2 The common ground stays the same: easy work is always the foundation.

In a 9-week study of trained endurance athletes, the polarised model produced larger improvements than training built around threshold, volume or high-intensity intervals.3

In practice

  • With 5 sessions a week: 4 easy, 1 quality. With 6–7 sessions you can go up to 2 quality sessions.
  • Easy sessions must be truly easy. If you can't talk, slow down: walking uphill is fine.
  • For the marathon race pace falls in zone 2: in the specific phase work at that pace increases, and the model becomes more pyramidal.
  • It applies to the bike too: long, easy rides count as zone 1 work in the week's total.
