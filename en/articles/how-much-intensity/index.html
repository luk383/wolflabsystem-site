Polarised or pyramidal: zones, counting and updated evidence

In brief

  • Polarised and pyramidal describe how work is distributed across three physiological zones. They are not individual workouts.
  • Time in zones and session counts are different measures: “80/20” needs a denominator.
  • Recent syntheses do not establish general polarised-training superiority for time-trial performance. Volume, level and purpose matter.

Three physiological zones, not your device zones

ZoneBoundary in the three-zone modelCaution
Z1Below first ventilatory or lactate thresholdNot automatically Z1 on a five-zone watch
Z2Between first and second thresholdsNot the “easy zone2” in every commercial scale
Z3Above second thresholdDoes not specify interval duration or recovery

Zone numbers depend on the model. Comparing percentages requires matching boundaries and measurement methods. Talking and perceived effort help in practice but do not replace threshold measurement when precision is needed. Marathon pace is not automatically assigned to the same zone for every runner.3

Pyramidal and polarised

In a pyramidal distribution, Z1 has the greatest share, followed by Z2 then Z3. In a polarised distribution, Z1 still dominates but Z3 exceeds Z2: the middle share is reduced. These labels describe an observed period; they do not require identical percentages every week.3

Arithmetic example onlyZ1Z2Z3
300 pyramidal minutes240 min (80%)45 min (15%)15 min (5%)
300 polarised minutes240 min (80%)15 min (5%)45 min (15%)

Both examples have 80% easy work but different stimuli: 80/20 alone does not distinguish pyramidal from polarised. These numbers explain distributions; they are not individual targets or loads to adopt.

Minutes and sessions are not interchangeable

In 2010, Seiler described national and international athletes completing 10–13 weekly sessions: about 80% of sessions were easy and 20% dominated by hard work. That does not prescribe 20% of minutes above threshold for everyone.1

A five-session example

Four easy sessions and one interval session are 80/20 by session count. If all last 60 minutes, the total is 300. If the hard session includes 15 minutes above the second threshold and 45 easy minutes, total time is 285 minutes Z1 and 15 Z3: 95/0/5, not 80/0/20. Warm-up, recoveries and cool-down change the count. Heart-rate counting is also affected by its delayed rise during short repeats.

State whether you count sessions by main purpose, time in heart-rate zones, or time at a given speed/power. Do not merge results calculated with different criteria.

What the research shows

SourceSample and designResult to interpret
Stöggl and Sperlich 201448 trained multi-sport athletes, 9 weeks; four groupsPOL: greatest VO₂peak increase (+11.7%) and other variables; no between-group economy difference
Oliveira et al. 2024Meta-analysis:17 studies, 437 participantsSmall POL advantage for VO₂peak; no superiority found for time trial, time to exhaustion or speed/power at second threshold
Rosenblat et al. 2025Individual-data network meta-analysis:13 studies, 348 participantsNo significant POL vs PYR difference for VO₂max or time trial using time in heart-rate zones

The 2014 result was from laboratory testing, not a race, in already trained people. The 2024 synthesis found the VO₂peak advantage mainly in interventions under twelve weeks and highly trained athletes. A VO₂peak gain does not automatically equal the same race-performance gain.23

The 2025 synthesis included 296 men and 52 women, 150 recreational and 198 competitive athletes. Level-based analysis suggested different responses: potentially greater aerobic benefits from POL in competitive athletes and PYR in recreational athletes. This subgroup result is not an algorithm that chooses a plan from the label “recreational”. The abstract found no significant overall difference between POL and the other models.4

The two meta-analyses are not simple replications: included studies, analyses and classification differ. A winner cannot be selected by adding one experiment’s percentages to pooled estimates.

Use it to read training

  • Choose a zone scale and counting method before calculating shares.
  • Look beyond one week: season phase and race specificity can change distribution.
  • Five sessions do not create an obligation for one or two hard sessions: recovery, experience and volume remain individual decisions.
  • Do not label a long session easy merely because it is long. Actual intensity and specific segments matter.
  • Count running and cycling separately before aggregating multisport training: studies use different protocols and populations.
