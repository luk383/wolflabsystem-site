In brief

  • On 18 November, 24 teams from the men's and women's WorldTour and the ProTeam categories signed the shareholder agreement of the Collective Cycling Group (CCG), a UK-based company owned by the teams themselves, to jointly develop and market commercial rights, data, media assets, merchandising, hospitality and fan experiences.1
  • It is not a breakaway league: the official statement says the CCG works "within the sport's framework, alongside its governing bodies, organisers and riders". The "Superliga" framing used by some media does not match the primary document.14
  • The official list of the 24 teams has not been published. EF Education, Canyon-SRAM and Pinarello-Q36.5 have confirmed publicly; according to Escape Collective, 13 of the 18 men's WorldTour teams and 10 of the 14 women's teams have signed, with big names like UAE absent and Visma unconfirmed but considered likely.2
  • The stated problem: cycling lives almost entirely off sponsors (about US$500 million a year for two decades, the CCG's figure), with no ticketing or shared broadcast revenue. The project director is Oliver Ciesla, a sports marketing executive with a motorsport background. First test: the UCI WorldTour seminar on 9-10 December.12

What the Collective Cycling Group is

The statement published on Wednesday 18 November announces the birth of Collective Cycling Group Ltd., a British company "initiated and owned by the collective of cycling teams involved". The purpose: to jointly monetise the commercial rights, data and media assets the teams already hold, plus merchandising, hospitality and fan experiences. Women's WorldTour teams are described as founding shareholders ("not an afterthought"). Talks with additional teams are ongoing.1

The official site lists five areas: safer racing, fan engagement, growth into new territories, common representation and financial sustainability. The diagnosis is explicit: major sports rest on three revenue streams (ticketing, broadcast, sponsorship), cycling on one. With one important caveat: races "must stay free to attend", but teams want collective weight to negotiate broadcast deals fairly.1

Who has confirmed, who is missing

The announcement does not include the list of the 24 teams. Several have shared the statement on their own channels, confirming their involvement: EF Education (men's and women's WorldTour squads), Canyon-SRAM and Pinarello-Q36.5. According to the Escape Collective reconstruction reported by Cyclingnews, 13 of the 18 men's WorldTour teams have signed — the absentees said to be UAE Team Emirates-XRG, Red Bull-Bora-Hansgrohe, Ineos, Jayco-AlUla and Groupama-FDJ United — and 10 of the 14 women's WorldTour teams, with UAE, FDJ United-Suez, Liv AlUla Jayco and Human Powered Health out. These figures are unofficial: we report them as journalism, not as CCG data.2

Why it is not a Super League (and what One Cycling has to do with it)

Some headlines, in Spain and elsewhere, called it cycling's "Superliga". The comparison with football's Super League suggests a closed breakaway competition: the primary document says the opposite — that the CCG works within the existing framework together with the UCI, organisers and riders, and that the ongoing UCI reform process "creates a first opportunity" to tackle the problems. The scope is commercial — rights, data, media, merchandising — not sporting: no alternative calendars or new races are announced.14

The project rises "from the ashes of One Cycling", Cyclingnews writes: the previous attempt to reform the sport's economic model, linked to Richard Plugge (Visma-Lease a Bike), who was initially a director of the new vehicle before leaving both company roles to Ciesla. Earlier still came the 2011 World Series Cycling, backed by the Rothschild group with 14 teams, and the Velon group in 2014, which kept to a narrower scope. The open question, Cyclingnews writes, is how the CCG will differ from those attempts.2

Why now, and what to watch

The backdrop is the fragility of the sponsor-only model: the CCG cites US$500 million a year from sponsors over two decades, and cases like TotalEnergies, saved at the last minute after its sponsor pulled out, show what happens when a single backer leaves. In parallel, the UCI has started its own reform: the results of a large stakeholder consultation — calendar, economic model, fan engagement, safety — were presented during the Montreal World Championships. How the UCI receives the CCG should become clearer at the WorldTour seminar on 9-10 December 2026.12

For the fan watching from home or the roadside, nothing changes immediately: the calendar, the races and the broadcasts stay as they are. If the project holds, the possible effects concern team stability and the content around the racing, not the format of the competitions.

Limitations

The team list is not published on the official site: the memberships cited are teams' own confirmations or the Escape Collective reconstruction via Cyclingnews. The economic figures (US$500 million a year) and the assessments of commercial potential come from the CCG itself, an interested party. The details of which specific rights will be marketed, and the economic governance among the teams, are not known. There are no official positions yet from the UCI or the major organisers (ASO, RCS, Flanders Classics). Ciesla's quotes come from the press release.
