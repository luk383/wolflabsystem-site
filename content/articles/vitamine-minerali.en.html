In brief

  • Vitamins and minerals are not ergogenic aids: they correct or prevent a confirmed deficiency, they do not make someone without one stronger.
  • The IOC asks for an assessment before supplementing: medical and diet history, tests, a check for drug interactions, and a baseline to measure progress. The "insurance policy" supplement is not the model it supports.
  • The nutrients most often at risk in athletes are iron, calcium and vitamin D. B12 is a separate case for people on a vegan or near-vegan diet.
  • High doses of single antioxidants, especially vitamins C and E, may blunt training adaptations. No doses here: an excess can do harm.

The principle

The IOC consensus separates correcting a deficiency from taking a supplement "just in case". A deficiency, even mild, can reduce training and performance, but it is hard to measure: for many nutrients there is no single threshold of "adequate". When status is low, a supplement can be part of treatment, together with a plan that is adequate in energy and nutrients.1 That is why there are no doses here: they depend on tests and on the person, and the assessment belongs to a doctor or sports dietitian.

Nutrients, one by one

  • Vitamin D: many athletes are at risk of insufficiency at various times of year. There is no consensus on the value that defines deficiency or sufficiency, and the need to supplement depends on sun exposure and skin type. Guidelines for athletes are not established; high doses need monitoring to avoid toxicity.1 The AIS lists vitamin D, iron, calcium, folate and zinc in Group A: for identified athletes with protocols, not for general use.2
  • Iron: low status can come from poor intake or absorption, too little energy, menstruation, red-cell breakdown from foot strike, losses in sweat or altitude training. It needs several measures together (for example ferritin, transferrin saturation, haemoglobin). High-dose iron should not be taken unless there is a deficiency, and a deficiency needs clinical follow-up.1
  • Calcium: risk rises with avoiding dairy and other calcium-rich foods, with restricted energy and with disordered eating. There is no good indicator of calcium status; vitamin D and bone health also matter.1
  • Vitamin B12, folate, iodine: the IOC says that for B12 (vegan or near-vegan diet), folate (women who might become pregnant) and iodine (low-iodine areas, no iodised salt) supplementing may be warranted, but these apply to the general population, not specifically to athletes.1
  • Vitamins C and E: high doses of single antioxidants may blunt training-induced adaptations. For vitamin C in periods of heavy exertion, a Cochrane review cited by the IOC, of 5 studies (598 people), shows about 50% fewer respiratory symptoms; no benefit if started after symptoms appear. It is unclear whether it blunts adaptation in very well-trained athletes.1

Before buying anything

  • Start with food and training load: too little energy intake is a cause of deficiency that no supplement fixes.
  • Suspect a deficiency? Tests and a doctor, not self-diagnosis. Too much iron or vitamin D carries risks.
  • We do not cover multivitamins: the sources read give no picture for performance.
  • Anti-doping and contamination: the WADA list changes every year and an unchecked product can contain banned substances.31 WOLF LAB does not certify products or batches and does not recommend brands.
