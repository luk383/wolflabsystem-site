In short

  • In already trained endurance athletes, adding strength work did not reduce VO₂max gains in the studies reviewed.
  • Heavy loads and plyometrics tend to improve running and cycling economy and, in many but not all studies, performance.
  • The studies do not fix frequency or timing: the table and numbers below are practical examples to adapt, not a validated protocol.

The fear of interference

For years people worried that strength work would "steal" endurance adaptations. A meta-analysis published online in October 2023 (2024 volume) brought some order: 59 studies, 1,346 healthy adults aged 18–50. Only in untrained people did combined training slightly reduce VO₂max gains. In trained or highly trained endurance athletes the effect didn't appear, though the authors say more studies on women and highly trained athletes are needed. There was a small interference on lower-body strength, in men but not in women.1

For an amateur marathoner or cyclist who trains consistently, the risk of losing aerobic engine is minimal.

What you gain

A systematic review of 24 studies (runners with at least 6 months of experience, time trials of 1.5 to 10 km) found that strength training tends to improve running economy (2–8%) and time-trial performance, usually without changing VO₂max. Results were not consistent: six studies saw no economy gain, and methodological quality was moderate to low (PEDro 4–6 out of 10). These are not marathon studies.2 Another review, on trained runners and cyclists, says running economy improves with either heavy or explosive strength work, while heavy strength work is recommended for cycling economy; the evidence of an added benefit is strongest for cycling.3

On top of that, stronger muscles and tendons cope better with rising mileage, although direct evidence on this point is weaker.

In practice

Teaching example: sets and reps do not come from a single source and should be adapted.

Squat or split squat3–4 × 4–6Load that leaves 1–2 reps in reserve

  • How often (a practical example, not a number set by the studies): many programmes use two sessions a week in the base phase and one to maintain in the specific weeks. The only study I read on this pattern is small (6 well-trained cyclists vs 6) and not about running.4 One session or lighter loads may still help: the studies do not rule it out, they did not measure it here.
  • When (adaptable suggestion): better away from key workouts. Studied finding: in a meta-analysis of healthy adults, explosive strength gains were smaller when strength and aerobic work shared a session, and not significantly different with at least 3 hours between them. It does not measure endurance performance or set a threshold for everyone. The "48 hours" before a long run or race-pace session is practical caution, with no source.
  • Getting started: two or three weeks with moderate loads to learn the movements, then build up.
  • Taper (a practical idea with no source, to adapt to you and your coach): many reduce or stop heavy lifting late in the build. "10 days" is an example, not a rule.
