In short

  • In already trained endurance athletes, adding strength work did not reduce VO₂max gains in the studies reviewed.
  • Running, cycling and swimming have different results and dedicated pages: Strength for Run, Strength for Ride, Strength for Swim.
  • The studies do not fix frequency or timing: the table and numbers below are practical examples to adapt, not a validated protocol.

The fear of interference

For years people worried that strength work would "steal" endurance adaptations. A meta-analysis published online in October 2023 (2024 volume) brought some order: 59 studies, 1,346 healthy adults aged 18–50. Only in untrained people did combined training slightly reduce VO₂max gains. In trained or highly trained endurance athletes the effect didn't appear, though the authors say more studies on women and highly trained athletes are needed. There was a small interference on lower-body strength, in men but not in women.1

For an amateur marathoner or cyclist who trains consistently, the risk of losing aerobic engine is minimal.

Where to look for your sport

How much strength work changes running economy, pedalling or swimming depends on the sport and the method, and cannot be read from a single figure. The data are on the dedicated pages: Strength for Run, Strength for Ride and Strength for Swim. This page keeps the general picture on interference.

On top of that, stronger muscles and tendons cope better with rising mileage, although direct evidence on this point is weaker.

  • How often (a practical example, not a number set by the studies): many programmes use two sessions a week in the base phase and one to maintain in the specific weeks. The only study I read on this pattern is small (6 well-trained cyclists vs 6) and not about running.2 These studies do not set a minimum frequency valid for everyone.
  • When (reasoned suggestion): better away from key workouts. Order, spacing between sessions and the studied data are in Session placement and fatigue. As a practical, non-numeric criterion: place strength so it does not leave tired legs for key sessions, and adjust it to fatigue and recovery.
  • Getting started: two or three weeks with moderate loads to learn the movements, then build up.
  • Approaching the race (reasoned suggestion, no direct evidence): lower the strength load as the race nears, based on fatigue and recovery, and add no new exercises. No study I read says when to stop.
