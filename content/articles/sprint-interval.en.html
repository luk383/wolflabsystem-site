In brief

  • Sprint interval training (SIT) and aerobic intervals are not synonyms: the described protocol uses maximal 30-second efforts, not a steady FTP percentage.
  • Gibala and colleagues’ 2006 study compared six sessions over two weeks in 16 young active men, not a season in trained cyclists.
  • Sprint minutes are few, but recovery and preparation count. The work is demanding and the study was supervised.

What kind of sprint?

Here SIT means repeated maximal 30-second cycle-ergometer efforts using a Wingate model. It is not a few-second finishing sprint or a controlled-intensity VO₂max interval set. The study used standardised ergometer resistance, familiarisation and supervision: its protocol cannot be transferred to the road by copying a wattage.1

Sessions took place on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays for two weeks, with one or two days between them. This describes the research, not a recommended frequency for the reader. Reported group mean power is not an individual target and is not converted to an FTP percentage.1

Two minutes of sprints is not a two-minute workout

Four 30-second repeats give two minutes of work. Counting four four-minute recoveries, as in the study table, gives an 18-minute block; six repeats give three work minutes and 27 minutes with six recoveries. The source reports 18–27 minutes including recovery, not just 2–3 minutes. This count does not include personally added warm-up or cool-down.1

Illustrative counting example

Counting recovery only between four repeats gives three recoveries: 2 + 3 × 4 = 14 minutes. Including recovery after the final sprint gives 18. This is not a contradiction to hide: the block boundaries differ. Reading a study or workout requires knowing what is included.

What improved

Both groups improved in fixed-work 50 and 750 kJ tests, with no between-group difference in the reported results. The authors also observed muscle adaptations including oxidative capacity, buffering and glycogen. Tests were laboratory-based: kJ were displayed as simulated distance, not road races.1

“Similar initial adaptations” does not make sprints and long rides interchangeable for every outcome. The paper itself warns that these data do not establish adequate preparation for prolonged endurance events through SIT alone.

Why it is not a universal plan

  • A small sample of young active men over two weeks does not automatically represent women, older people, sedentary people or elite athletes.
  • The protocol was maximal and supervised; little time does not mean little effort or little recovery need.
  • The research does not determine how to add these sprints to an already high running, cycling or strength load.
  • The table summarises a studied protocol. It does not prescribe maximal efforts or replace suitability assessment, technique, warm-up and safe conditions.
  • The authors leave responses over many weeks or months unresolved. Chronic effects are not promised from a fourteen-day result.
