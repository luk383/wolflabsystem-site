At a glance

  • In a prospective study of 339 adult runners from 24 countries, those reporting poorer sleep quality faced a higher risk of running-related injury: hazard ratio 1.36, meaning a 36% higher risk for each extra point on the poorer-sleep scale.1
  • In the two weeks before a layoff, muscle soreness runs higher than usual; perceived fatigue rises in the week immediately before.1
  • Training load, perceived stress, sleep duration and sleep latency show no significant association. The study is observational: it describes an association and does not prove that sleeping better prevents injuries.1

How the study is built

The work by Mathias Goldberg and colleagues appears in the Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports (2025). During a collection window between March 2022 and December 2023, the researchers followed adult runners with at least one year of experience and at least two weekly sessions: every Sunday, through a dedicated app, participants reported any injuries and completed a short questionnaire on sleep (quality, duration, time to fall asleep), fatigue, muscle soreness and stress. Training load came from connected watches or Strava accounts.1

The final sample is 339 runners, 83.8% men, average age 43, almost all (92.9%) regular competitors; 81.7% were based in France. An injury counted if it limited running for at least seven days or three consecutive sessions, or required a medical consultation.1

What it found

In the time-to-event analysis, sleep quality is the only factor significantly associated with injury risk (HRR 1.36; 95% confidence interval 1.04-1.78; p=0.02): each extra point on the poorer-sleep score corresponds to a 36% higher risk. Sleep duration and latency show no comparably clear association, nor do training load and stress.1

In the week-by-week analysis of 325 runners, soreness was higher both two weeks before (p=0.02) and in the week immediately preceding the injury (p<0.001), while fatigue rises only in the week right before (p=0.04). These are possible early warning signs, not a diagnosis.1

What it means in practice

The cautious reading suggested by the authors themselves: sleep quality, soreness and fatigue can act as warning lights to watch week by week, within a broader approach to injury-risk management. It is not a protocol: the study does not show that improving sleep reduces injuries, because unmeasured factors could drive both. The authors also excluded watch sleep metrics as not reliable enough across brands: all sleep data here are self-assessments.12

Limitations

This article is based on the study text (DOI 10.1111/sms.70164, also available through the HAL open archive) and on the Physle Daily summary of 21 November, both read on 24 November. The main caveats, stated by the authors: an observational design with no causal inference; high dropout (from 1,143 to 339 analysed athletes, with an actual average follow-up of 4.2 weeks against a planned 26-week window); injuries and recovery all self-reported without medical diagnosis; previous injuries, a known predictor, not assessable due to missing data; a predominantly male, French and competitive sample, which limits generalisability. This is not medical advice: your own doctor remains the reference for health questions.
