A different kind of debut

An international athletics career does not remove the uncertainty of a first marathon. That is the interesting part of Davide Re's story, reported by Tuttosport on 20 November: the two-time Olympian, who set an Italian 400-metre record of 44.77 in 2019, approaches Turin as a newcomer to the distance.1

In the newspaper's account, his route to 42.195 km began with a friend's suggestion to run a half marathon in Milan in 2025. The 30 km Cortina-Dobbiaco and another event over that distance at Reggia di Venaria followed. This is not a direct jump from one lap of a track to the marathon. There were intermediate steps, although the sources we read do not provide a public training diary.1

Consistency before the clock

Re describes fitting training around university and family commitments. He initially worked with Thomas Cordazzi, then managed his sessions himself because he could not keep to the schedules. That is his personal experience as reported by Tuttosport, not advice to stop working with a coach.1

His initial goal was to break three and a half hours. After a less consistent period, Re says he found some regularity again in the final month and remains confident about his time goal. The source does not announce a slower revised target, so we do not attribute a decision to abandon sub-3:30 to him. Re identifies the 33rd kilometre as the point beyond which he does not yet know what to expect.1

Long-run fatigue through a sprinter's eyes

The contrast with 400 metres is primarily about perception. In Tuttosport's account, Re distinguishes the muscular difficulty of sprinting from the concentration required during runs lasting more than two hours. It is a concrete account of changing habits, not a physiological explanation that applies to everyone. One athlete's testimony cannot establish how fatigue mechanisms compare between the two events.1

For a first-time marathon runner, the useful point is not to copy his times or distances. It is that even an experienced athlete faces an unfamiliar distance, the need for consistent work and limits on available time. For training methods, see the sources and caveats in our marathon preparation guide, rather than an assumed Re training plan that we do not have.

The Turin race

Torino City Marathon takes place on Sunday 29 November 2026. The race document published on the FIDAL website schedules an 08:30 start in Piazza Vittorio Veneto and a finish in Piazza Castello, subject to organisational requirements. The official homepage confirms the date. These are programme details, not results or a final guarantee that any individual athlete will start.23
