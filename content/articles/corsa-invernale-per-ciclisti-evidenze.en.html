At a glance

  • On bone, there is "concerning but inconsistent, limited-quality" evidence that road cyclists may have low bone mineral density, particularly at the lumbar spine; in one small Norwegian elite sample, 10 of 19 cyclists had low BMD and none of the 21 runners did, despite all reporting heavy resistance training. No study, however, shows that adding running improves bone.12
  • On load, the only available proxy is novice runners: progressing weekly distance by more than 30% over 2 weeks is associated with more "distance-related" injuries (HR 1.59, p=0.07, an exploratory study), yet in an RCT a graduated programme built on the 10% rule did not reduce injuries.34
  • In the literature reviewed for this piece we found no studies that directly observe cyclists who start running: the "trained aerobic engine, impact-unready structures" asymmetry is plausible physiological reasoning, not a measured finding.

Why it comes up

In winter many cyclists cut back on riding and add running: it takes little time, works in any weather and keeps the aerobic stimulus going. The common reasoning adds a second argument: cycling is a "supported" sport, where the saddle carries the weight, and running would give bones the impact loading that cycling does not. It is worth asking what the literature actually says, pillar by pillar.

Bone: the most concrete point, not a guarantee

A systematic review by Nagle and Brooks (Sports Health, 2011) examined 13 studies on cyclists and bone mineral density (BMD): 11 cross-sectional and only 2 prospective. The authors' conclusion is that the evidence is "concerning but inconsistent and of limited quality": road cyclists may be at risk of low bone mass, particularly at the lumbar spine. In the two prospective studies, Barry and Kohrt followed 14 amateur male cyclists for a year and observed BMD declining over the season (−0.7% at the femoral neck), with hip BMD 1.5% below baseline at 12 months; Beshgetoor and colleagues, in middle-aged women followed for 18 months, saw lumbar spine BMD maintained in runners but declining in cyclists and sedentary controls.1

A more recent cross-sectional study of Norwegian national elite athletes (Klomsten Andersen et al., BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine, 2018) points the same way: among 40 athletes, the 19 cyclists had lower BMD than the 21 runners at every measured site; 10 of 19 cyclists (53%) were classified with low BMD (Z-score ≤−1) at the lumbar spine or femoral neck, against none of the runners, despite reporting heavy lower-body resistance training. The limits matter: a small convenience sample, national elites only, cross-sectional design; it does not generalise to amateurs.2

The honest reading: that gym strength work alone did not prevent low BMD in that elite sample is a finding; that adding winter running improves an amateur cyclist's bone remains a plausible hypothesis, not something demonstrated by any of the studies reviewed.

Load: weak evidence in both directions

With no studies on running progression in people coming from the bike, the closest proxy is the novice-runner literature, and even there the picture is less sharp than common wisdom suggests. Nielsen and colleagues (JOSPT, 2014) followed 874 beginners for a year with GPS watches: those progressing weekly distance by more than 30% over 2 weeks showed more "distance-related" injuries (patellar tendinopathy, iliotibial band syndrome, medial tibial stress syndrome and others) than those staying under 10%, with a hazard ratio of 1.59 but a confidence interval of 0.96-2.66 and p=0.07: a fragile statistical margin, in a study the authors themselves call exploratory and in need of RCT confirmation.3

On the other side, the GRONORUN RCT by Buist and colleagues (American Journal of Sports Medicine, 2008), in 532 beginners, compared a graduated 13-week programme built on the 10% rule with a standard 8-week programme: no difference in injuries (20.8% versus 20.3%, p=0.90). The prudent summary is neither "a magic progression exists" nor "progression does not matter": sudden jumps are associated with some injury types, no percentage rule has solid proof, and monitoring the individual response remains the most defensible principle.4

The gap to declare

In the literature reviewed for this article we found no studies that directly observe cyclists who start running: not on bone, not on injuries, not on aerobic transfer. That does not prove none exist anywhere; it means this piece cannot pretend they do. The often-cited asymmetry, an aerobic engine already trained with tendons and bones unused to impact, is plausible physiology and should be treated as reasoning, not as a finding measured in this specific transition.

How to read it in practice

Three consequences consistent with the evidence. First: if bone is the goal, running is a reasonable candidate but no guarantee, and strength work alone, in the elite sample cited, was not enough. Second: individual early-warning signs matter more than general rules; the sleep-and-injury study published yesterday points to soreness and fatigue rising in the two weeks before a layoff, within the limits of an observational design. Third: someone coming from the bike has the lungs but not the impact calluses, which suggests early caution as a prudent principle, not as a numerical threshold. Limitations

This article is based on four primary sources read on 25 November: the Nagle & Brooks 2011 systematic review (full text), the Klomsten Andersen 2018 study (full text), and the PubMed abstracts of Nielsen 2014 and Buist 2008. The main limits: the bone evidence is largely cross-sectional and of quality its own authors call limited; the Norwegian study covers elite athletes, not amateurs; Nielsen's study is exploratory with marginal significance; Buist's RCT covers previously sedentary novice runners, not cyclists. None of the sources directly observes the bike-to-run transition: implications for the amateur cyclist are inferences, declared as such. This is not medical advice: your own doctor remains the reference for health questions.
