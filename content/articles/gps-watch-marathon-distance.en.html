In short

  • At the finish of a marathon, many watches show more than 42.195 km. In most cases that does not mean the course was long: your watch and the official measurement are measuring two different things.1,2
  • Official courses are measured by qualified measurers along the "running line": the shortest possible route staying on the road, at a constant distance from the kerbs. The only method approved by World Athletics and AIMS is the calibrated bicycle.1,2
  • In a race, among thousands of people, wide corners, aid stations and overtaking, following the ideal line is nearly impossible: you run those extra metres, and the GPS records them.1
  • Watch error goes both ways: tall buildings, tree cover and crowds degrade the satellite signal, and the final figure can come out shorter or longer than reality. A watch alone neither certifies nor condemns a course.1

What your watch really measures

Your watch or app does not measure the road: it reconstructs your position from satellite signals, point by point, and adds up the distances. It is a fine tool for logging training and reading pace, but it is an estimate, not a certified measurement. As the support pages of Great Run, the UK's biggest road-racing series, explain, it is not unusual for a device to record a distance shorter or longer than the official race distance.1

Accuracy gets worse precisely in typical city-marathon conditions: tall buildings reflecting the signal, tree cover, large groups of people. The result is a track that widens, cuts corners or "bounces": kilometres that appear or disappear without you ever running them.1

How the official course is measured

An official race course is not measured by GPS or by car: a qualified measurer measures it with a calibrated bicycle, the only method approved by World Athletics and AIMS, the association of marathons and distance road races worldwide. Before the survey, the bicycle is calibrated over a precisely measured reference section; the measurer then rides the course following the "running line".2

The running line is defined by international standards: it is the shortest possible route while staying on the road, keeping a constant distance from the kerbs. In practice it is the line an ideal runner would follow, taking every corner as tight as possible. AIMS races, the association explains, are measured by accredited Grade A or B measurers precisely so that runners can trust the distance.1,2

Why in a race you run more than the ideal line

The certified 42.195 km exists only along the running line. You, however, do not run on that line: you start in the middle of thousands of people, swing wide on corners to overtake, drift towards aid stations, cross the road to find space. Every metre of deviation from the ideal line adds up, and over 42 kilometres small continuous widenings are enough to add a few hundred metres. As Great Run notes, when you run with many others it is not always possible to stay on the measured line.1

So the two numbers coexist without contradicting each other: the certified course is 42.195 km along the shortest line; your watch recorded your actual route, longer, with satellite-signal noise on top.1

What your GPS does not prove

Beware the symmetric reasoning: if your watch shows 42.6 km it does not prove the course was long, and if it showed 41.8 it would not prove it was short. Device error can go in either direction, and a single consumer-grade reading can neither clear nor condemn the official measurement, which rests on calibrated-bicycle surveys and federation checks. If a serious doubt about a course's length ever arises, the path is a technical review by the competent bodies, not a screenshot of your own activity.1,2

Limits

  • This article explains how official measurement and GPS error work; it does not assess the length of any specific course and is no substitute for technical checks by federations.
  • We give no numeric estimate of "typical" GPS error: it depends on the device, the environment and the day's conditions, and the primary sources we consulted provide no updated figures.1
  • Certification rules can differ in detail between countries and governing bodies; here we describe the international principle (calibrated bicycle, shortest line), not any single federation's operating manual.2
