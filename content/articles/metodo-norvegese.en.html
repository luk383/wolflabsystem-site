In short

  • "Norwegian method" is used for several things: lactate-guided threshold sessions, days with two threshold sessions, and the very high volume of easy running around them. They are not synonyms.
  • The base is observational: it describes how some world-class runners (1500-5000 m) train. No controlled study has tested that it works better than other models.
  • For amateurs there is no direct evidence. Copying volumes, double sessions or a "standard" lactate value is the easiest way to get it wrong.

Three different things under one name

  • Lactate-guided threshold sessions. The pace is not fixed in advance: you aim for a lactate concentration and adjust speed. Casado and colleagues (2023) call them lactate-guided threshold interval training.1
  • Double threshold. Two threshold sessions in the same day, usually morning and evening, with easy running on the days around it.23
  • High volume of easy running. Threshold does not replace easy work: it is added on top of a large amount of slow kilometres.1

Why it might work (hypotheses, not proof)

Casado and colleagues suggest that working in a lactate zone lower than VO₂max intensity causes less fatigue, and so allows more specific work across the week. They also point to studies of paces near the lactate threshold in trained runners.1 These are plausible explanations, presented by the authors as hypotheses.

Bakken adds his own reading: the limit on load would be mostly muscular, and grouping threshold work into double days leaves more recovery days. This is an empirical opinion from someone who practised it, not a research result.3

What we do not know

  • No controlled study. Casado and colleagues write that the efficacy of the model has not been tested in controlled studies, and that their assumptions should be taken with caution.1 The Kelemen review describes volumes and distribution; it does not show an advantage over other models.2
  • Elite only, 1500-5000 m only. The same authors say the model has been described in 1500 m and 5000 m runners, and that its application to other events, such as the marathon, remains uncertain.1
  • Lactate is individual. Bakken reports thresholds between 2.0 and 3.0 mmol/L in different athletes and a personal value of about 3.0 for himself. One number does not fit everyone.3 Zone limits should be found with tests on the individual athlete (Casado and colleagues).1
  • Success and method are two things. The results of the runners cited do not prove the method is the cause: the sources do not separate the contribution of talent, volume, altitude and organisation.

For an amateur

There is no evidence in amateurs. What follows is the opinion of experts in a Runner's World feature, not the result of a study.4

  • Christopher Harnish, an exercise physiologist, doubts that double-threshold days are necessary or wise for most non-professional runners: elite runners have built the capacity to sustain large volumes and can recover like professionals; most of us cannot.
  • Carl Foster, co-author of the 2023 paper, says very hard work can still be useful but not more than once a week if you also do threshold days, and that threshold should be retested three or four times a year.
  • As a cautious first step, Harnish suggests one threshold session in the morning, for example 3 × 8 minutes with 4 minutes rest, and a 30-minute walk instead of the second session. This is an expert suggestion, not a tested protocol.

Before changing your plan: intensity must be described with one consistent method (lactate, heart rate or feel), your easy running volume must already hold up, and total load must stay compatible with recovery. Lactate needs equipment and testing; without it, "threshold pace" is an estimate. This page is not a recommendation to change your plan.

Where it sits next to pyramidal and polarised

Lactate-guided threshold sits in the middle zone of the three-zone model. So the Norwegian method is neither "polarised" nor "pyramidal" by definition: it depends on how time in zones is counted. For definitions and counting limits see How much intensity do you need.
