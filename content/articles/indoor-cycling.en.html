In brief

  • The 2019 systematic review shows improvements in VO₂max, blood pressure and some metabolic markers; its own authors note that only one of the included studies was a randomised trial.
  • The most solid practical advantage is intensity control: in one validation of a single smart trainer model, power stayed within about 1% of target. One model and ten cyclists: the result does not extend to every trainer.
  • Heat and the absence of airflow greatly reduce exercise tolerance indoors: a fan is the change with the most direct evidence.

What we mean

Indoor cycling means pedalling indoors: exercise bikes, a bike on rollers or a smart trainer, and group classes in gyms. The reviews include studies that differ widely in equipment, duration and participants, so results should be read outcome by outcome, not as a single verdict on training indoors.1

What the reviews say about benefits

A 2019 systematic review collected 13 studies and 372 participants, mostly women, with the search updated to January 2019. The authors themselves flag the main limit: among the included studies there was only one randomised controlled trial.1

For lipids, weight and blood pressure, combining indoor cycling + diet outperformed exercise alone: indoor riding by itself is not presented as a self-sufficient weight-loss intervention.1

A more recent systematic review confirms the physiological picture: indoor cycling sessions consistently reach high intensities, with examples at 80–90% of maximum heart rate, and produce improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness and some cardiometabolic markers. The authors stress the heterogeneity of the studies and that the environment (temperature, humidity, sweating) significantly influences how intense the same session becomes.2

Heat and airflow: the most underrated physical factor

On the road, movement itself creates airflow; indoors it does not. In a time-to-exhaustion cycling study at 30 °C and 50% humidity, riding with a 30 km/h airflow lasted about 90 minutes versus 41 without airflow, and core temperature rose faster without ventilation. Above an air speed of roughly 10 km/h, thermal strain fell measurably.3

It is the study with the most direct evidence on a practical point: a fan is not a comfort, it is part of the equipment. Without one, the same power becomes more costly and the session shortens because of heat, not muscular work.

Measurable power: the control advantage

The strength of smart trainers is measurement. In one validation study, a smart trainer model stayed within about 1% of the power target, with good agreement against power-meter pedals between 100 and 270 W. One model, one study, ten cyclists: the result does not extend to every trainer, but shows indoor power can be a reliable measure on which to build target sessions.4

This is what makes indoor different from the road: no traffic lights, descents or wind to interrupt the intervals. Setting targets needs a threshold and its zones: see FTP and power zones and VO₂max intervals on the bike.

Virtual platforms and motivation

In a randomised crossover study with 31 participants, outdoor cycling beat virtual reality for enjoyment, motivation and intention to repeat the experience; in turn, virtual reality increased engagement compared with traditional indoor cycling without scenery. At the same perceived effort, heart rate outdoors was lower.5

An honest reading: virtual platforms make indoor riding more sustainable, not equivalent to riding outside. If the problem is consistency, they are a tool with some evidence in their favour; if sold as a complete substitute for the road, the available data say otherwise.

What is only marketing

  • "Burn up to 1,000 calories a class": expenditure depends on power, duration and the person; none of the cited reviews supports fixed numbers.
  • "Lose weight with the bike alone": the strongest evidence on weight and lipids covers exercise plus diet, not exercise alone.1
  • "Like the road, but better": the direct comparison on motivation and enjoyment favours outdoor; indoor wins on control and repeatability, which are different things.5

Limits

  • The research base is small and heterogeneous: only one RCT in the main review, often small and mostly female samples, very different protocols.1
  • The reported percentages come from the cited studies and their contexts (people with metabolic syndrome, months-long programmes): they are not individual expectations.
  • The smart trainer validation covers a single model and ten athletes: it does not certify every trainer on the market.4
  • The high-intensity sessions (80–90% of max heart rate) described by the reviews are not universal advice: they load and require progression and recovery like any hard work.2
